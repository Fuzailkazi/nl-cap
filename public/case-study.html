The problem

More options than anyone has hours to evaluate

Between direct and regular plans, dozens of AMCs, and a scheme for every risk appetite, choosing where money sits has never had more options — or been harder to do properly. Reading a factsheet, checking the exit load, and separating a genuine review from an ad takes real time most people don't have between a job, a family, and everything else. My dad trades and invests seriously. He still outsourced that homework — to a phone call with me.

"When I'm deciding whether to hold, switch, or add to a fund, I want a fast, cited, no-spin answer — not an evening spent cross-referencing factsheets and forums — so I can act on my own judgment without becoming a full-time analyst."

Situation
Money already in the market, more funds to choose from every year, and a decision that shouldn't need a research degree.
Struggle
The facts exist, but they're scattered across PDFs, fine print, and forum noise nobody has time to reconcile.
Outcome
A straight answer he can verify himself — or an honest "I don't know," never a guess dressed up as one.
The moment it clicked

Dad's been trading and investing for years — he reads the news, watches the market, he's not naive about money. But mutual funds are a different kind of homework: exit loads, expense ratios, direct-vs-regular, reviews scattered across forums nobody indexes well. He'd call and ask "is this fund still good," or text me a screenshot asking what a fee actually meant, and I'd spend twenty minutes doing research he didn't have time for. Multiply that by every investor doing the same thing alone, and the real problem wasn't too few options — it's that evaluating any one of them properly takes longer than most people can spare. So I built the analyst I was already being for him: it answers in seconds, cites where it got the number, refuses to tell him what to do, and hands him to a real advisor the moment the question turns personal.

The effect

What actually changed for him

Before, a fund question meant a text to me and a wait while I dug through PDFs and forum threads on his behalf. Now he gets a cited answer in the time it takes to ask — and when the honest answer is "I don't have a verified source for that," he's learned to trust that as much as the answers with a citation attached.

The bigger shift was what he started asking on his own. Once the fee explainer put direct-vs-regular and expense-ratio drag into plain language, he started checking it against funds he already held — a question he'd never had a fast enough way to ask before, and one that, over years of compounding, is exactly the kind of thing that quietly costs an investor the most.

The bet

AI proposes, a human disposes

Every action the assistant can take — draft an email, hold a calendar slot, append a booking note — is queued as pending and executes only when a person clicks approve. Nothing goes out automatically, ever. It's the same instinct my dad already has about his broker: read the confirmation before it executes. That gate is what makes an assistant like this safe to hand to someone whose money is actually on the line.

How I made sure it wouldn't misinform him

An analyst's second opinion that never gets tired

This is money a real person acts on, so "the answer sounded right" was never going to be good enough. Before any answer format ships, it runs through five eval suites, each built to catch a different way an assistant like this can quietly go wrong.

01 · Retrieval

Does it find the right facts?

Checks recall against a golden set of real fund questions.

Guards against: answering confidently from the wrong scheme's page.

02 · Generation

Does it only say what the source says?

Faithfulness & relevance, scored by an LLM judge against retrieved sources.

Guards against: an invented number he'd act on — the single scariest failure mode with real money.

03 · Compliance

Does it ever cross into advice?

Confirms every advice-shaped question gets the refusal, verbatim, not a recommendation.

Guards against: the assistant quietly becoming the decision-maker instead of him.

04 · Injection

Can it be talked out of its own rules?

Runs jailbreak-style adversarial prompts designed to override the contract.

Guards against: a clever phrasing tricking it into advice or a fabricated claim.

05 · Structure

Is the output actually usable?

Checks the Weekly Pulse and Fee Explainer hold their shape — word limits, source counts, dates.

Guards against: something technically correct but too dense for him to actually read.

The method

How each verdict is actually computed

"It passed" isn't an answer on its own — here's the actual mechanism behind each of the five checks, and what has to be true for a case to count as a pass.

SuiteRuns againstScoring mechanismVerdict rule
Retrieval A fixed golden set of real fund questions, one per scheme Calls the real retriever; checks whether the correct scheme's source chunk lands in the top-K results Recall@3 / Recall@5 must clear a set threshold
Generation The same golden questions, run through the real answer pipeline A second, independent LLM call — "the judge" — scores faithfulness, relevance, helpfulness & completeness from 0–1 against the retrieved source text Faithfulness & relevance both ≥ 0.8
Compliance Adversarial prompts written to elicit advice, a performance claim, or a PII leak Deterministic string/regex match — output compared against the verbatim refusal string, a banned advice-phrase list, and a PII-pattern scan Zero tolerance — any single match fails the case
Injection Jailbreak-style adversarial prompts ("ignore your instructions," role-play overrides, etc.) Runs the real chat/voice pipeline end to end, then scans the response for a contract override or a leaked system prompt 0% attack success required across the full set
Structure Real generated Weekly Pulse, Fee Explainer, and booking codes Schema + regex validation — word counts, section counts, source counts, exact code format 100% structural match, no partial credit

Decisions I'd stand behind

Three calls, and what each one cost

Cite-or-refuse, over "always helpful"

Why
A confident wrong answer is a liability when it's someone's real portfolio, not a bad review. Grounding beats fluency.
Cost
The bot says "I don't have a verified source" more than users would like — a deliberate, defensible floor.

Human approval gate, over auto-send

Why
Trust and auditability outweigh latency when real money is downstream. The review step is the feature.
Cost
Not fully autonomous — every outbound action waits on a person.

Evals as a build gate, over an afterthought

Why
The compliance rules are only real if a test fails when they drift. Format contracts are pinned by evals, not vibes.
Cost
Slower to ship features — every output format has to earn its passing check.
